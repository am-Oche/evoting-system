CHAPTER ONE 
INTRODUCTION 
1.1 Background of Study 
Elections form the foundation of democratic governance, enabling citizens to choose leaders and 
influence public policy. However, traditional voting methods in Nigeria face serious challenges 
that undermine electoral credibility and public trust. During th e 2023 general elections, the 
Bimodal Voter Accreditation System (BVAS) experienced failures in approximately 14% of 
polling units, causing delays, voter disenfranchisement, and voting suspensions (Ene Obi, 2023; 
olufemiajasa, 2023). Additional problems in cluded late poll openings, missing materials, vote -
buying, ballot-box snatching, violence, and failure to upload results (Ene Obi, 2023). 
Electronic voting (e-voting) systems offer digital platforms for casting, counting, and transmitting 
votes efficiently. E-voting aims to improve efficiency, enhance transparency, reduce human error, 
and strengthen public confidence in electoral outcomes (A desiyan, 2023). Estonia successfully 
implemented internet -based voting in 2005 using national ID cards for secure authentication, 
demonstrating that digital platforms can increase efficiency whilst maintaining security (Fabian 
Breuer and Alexander H. Trechse, 2006; David Mac Dougall, 2023). 
However, conventional e-voting systems face significant limitations. Centralized architectures are 
vulnerable to cyberattacks, data manipulation, and limited auditability. Poor infrastructure, 
inadequate training, and connectivity issues further compromise effectiveness in Nigeria (Ene Obi, 
2023). 
Blockchain technology addresses these limitations through decentralized distributed ledgers that 
ensure immutability, transparency, and tamper -resistance. Blockchain -based e -voting systems 
enable verifiable vote records, prevent double voting, preserve vot er anonymity, and provide 
auditable election trails (Russo et al., 2021; Ohize et al., 2024). 
Integrating blockchain e-voting with Nigeria's National Identification Number (NIN) strengthens 
voter authentication and reduces impersonation. The NIN, issued by the National Identity 
Management Commission (NIMC), provides unique identifiers for Nigerian citizens, enabling 
secure voter verification (Adesiyan, 2023).

This study designs and implements a blockchain -enabled e -voting platform linked to NIN to 
address Nigeria's recurring electoral challenges including technical failures, authentication issues, 
and public mistrust in the Independent National Electoral Commis sion (INEC). The system aims 
to enhance the integrity and reliability of Nigerian electoral processes (Ene Obi, 2023; Adesiyan, 
2023). 
1.2 Statement of Problem  
Elections in Nigeria are central to democratic governance, yet persistent challenges undermine 
their credibility. During the 2023 general elections, approximately 14% of polling units 
experienced failures in the Bimodal Voter Accreditation System (BVAS), l eading to delayed 
voting, disenfranchisement, and suspension of voting at affected units (Ene Obi, 2023). Election 
observers reported missing materials, late opening of polling units, ballot -box snatching, 
intimidation, and failure to upload results for mu ltiple locations (Ene Obi, 2023; olufemiajasa, 
2023). 
While electronic and biometric systems like BVAS have been introduced to improve efficiency 
and transparency, centralized architectures remain vulnerable to technical failures, manipulation, 
and limited auditability. Connectivity issues, power outages, and  human error continue to 
compromise system reliability (Adesiyan, 2023). 
Despite these interventions, no existing blockchain -based system integrated with the National 
Identification Number (NIN) exists in Nigeria to ensure secure, verifiable, and tamper -proof 
elections. This gap highlights the need for an innovative solution co mbining decentralized ledger 
technology with NIN authentication to guarantee transparency, auditability, and trust in electoral 
outcomes. This study designs and implements a blockchain-enabled e-voting system using NIN to 
address these systemic deficiencies and enhance the integrity of future Nigerian elections (Russo 
et al., 2021; Ene Obi, 2023).

CHAPTER TWO 
LITERATURE REVIEW 
2.1 Introduction to the Research Topic 
Electoral integrity remains a critical challenge in developing nations, where vote manipulation, 
fraud, and lack of transparency undermine public confidence in democratic processes (Kshetri & 
V oas, 2018). Blockchain-based e-voting systems offer a solution by leveraging blockchain's key 
features decentralization, immutability, transparency, and cryptographic security to create tamper-
resistant voting infrastructures that can be audited by multiple stakeholders (Hjálmarsson et al., 
2018). 
Integrating Nigeria's National Identification Number (NIN) adds a crucial layer of identity 
verification. This ensures "one person, one vote" by preventing duplicate registrations and voter 
impersonation (Yaga et al., 2019). This research combines blockchain technology for secure vote 
recording with NIN-based authentication to create a verifiable electoral platform. 
The system addresses Nigeria's specific challenges, manual vote counting across dispersed polling 
units, electoral malpractices like ballot stuffing, and the need for secure yet inclusive voting 
mechanisms (Adeniji & Olaosebikan, 2020). By enabling real-time vote counting and verification, 
the system can enhance electoral credibility, reduce disputes, and restore citizen confidence that 
votes are accurately recorded without manipulation (Pawlak et al., 2018). 
2.2 Historical Context of the Research Topic 
Electronic voting systems have evolved through several distinct phases over five decades. 
Traditional paper-based voting relies on manual counting and physical ballot boxes, making it 
vulnerable to manipulation, human error, and logistical challenges (Grit zalis, 2002). The first 
generation of electronic voting emerged in the 1960s with direct recording electronic (DRE) 
machines using push buttons or touch screens. However, these systems lacked verifiable paper 
trails and raised concerns about result manipulation (Mercuri, 2002). 
The 1990s introduced internet -based voting systems. Estonia implemented remote e -voting for 
national elections, but these centralized systems remained vulnerable to server attacks and lacked 
transparency (Springall et al., 2014). The breakthrough came in 2 008 when Bitcoin introduced 
7 
 
blockchain technology, demonstrating how distributed ledgers could create tamper -resistant 
records without central authority (Nakamoto, 2008). Researchers began exploring blockchain for 
voting in the early 2010s, recognizing its potential to address electoral integrity challenges (Ayed, 
2017). 
The first blockchain voting trials occurred between 2016 -2018 in Switzerland, South Korea, and 
Sierra Leone, though these implementations faced scalability and usability challenges 
(Hjálmarsson et al., 2018). In Nigeria, the National Identity Management Co mmission (NIMC) 
launched the NIN system in 2014, creating the infrastructure necessary for digital identity 
verification in e -voting (NIPC, 2020). Recent research focuses on integrating biometric 
authentication with blockchain voting to strengthen identity  verification while maintaining ballot 
secrecy (Alam et al., 2021). Challenges around voter privacy, accessibility, and regulatory 
frameworks continue to drive ongoing research. 
2.2.1 Theoretical Frameworks 
This research applies several established frameworks from information security and democratic 
systems. The CIA Triad (Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability) provides foundational security 
principles (Stallings & Brown, 2018). Confidentiality ensures vot es remain secret and cannot be 
linked to voters. Integrity guarantees votes cannot be altered after submission. Availability ensures 
eligible voters can access the system during voting periods. 
The Defense -in-Depth model informs the multi -layered security approach. Blockchain 
immutability, cryptographic encryption, NIN authentication, and smart contract validation 
function as complementary layers protecting against different attack vectors (Scarf one & Mell, 
2007). The Zero Trust Security model requires continuous verification throughout the voting 
process rather than relying only on initial login (Rose et al., 2020). V oters authenticate using NIN 
before accessing ballots, votes are cryptographically signed to prevent tampering, and blockchain 
nodes independently verify each transaction. These frameworks guide the system architecture 
towards comprehensive security while maintaining ballot secrecy and electoral transparency.

2.3 Key Concepts and Definitions 
i. Blockchain: is a distributed ledger technology that records transactions across multiple 
computers, making records immutable and transparent. Each block contains a 
cryptographic hash of the previous block, creating a tamper -evident chain (Yaga et al., 
2019). 
ii. Smart Contracts: are self-executing programs stored on a blockchain that automatically 
enforce predefined rules and conditions, enabling automated vote validation and tallying 
without human intervention (Zheng et al., 2020). 
iii. Decentralization: describes a system architecture where control and decision-making are 
distributed across multiple nodes rather than concentrated in a single central authority, 
reducing single points of failure and manipulation risk (Buterin, 2014). 
iv. Immutability: means that once data is recorded on a blockchain, it cannot be altered or 
deleted without detection, ensuring votes remain permanent and tamper -proof after 
submission (Nakamoto, 2008). 
v. Consensus Mechanism : refers to the algorithm blockchain networks use to achieve 
agreement among distributed nodes on transaction validity. Common mechanisms include 
Proof of Work, Proof of Stake, and Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance, is a system that 
allows a network to re ach a correct agreement even if up to one -third of its members are 
lying or failing, by having all nodes cross-verify each other's messages in multiple rounds. 
(Mingxiao et al., 2017). 
vi. National Identification Number (NIN): is a unique 11-digit number issued by Nigeria's 
National Identity Management Commission to citizens and legal residents, serving as the 
primary identifier for government services and identity verification (NIMC, 2020). 
vii. Cryptographic Hashing: uses mathematical algorithms to convert data into fixed -length 
strings that uniquely represent the original data, enabling vote verification without 
revealing vote content (Stallings & Brown, 2018). 
viii. Digital Signature: is a cryptographic technique that authenticates the sender of a digital 
message and ensures message integrity, allowing voters to sign their ballots without 
revealing their identity (Katz & Lindell, 2014). 
9 
 
ix. Electoral Integrity : refers to the degree to which elections are conducted fairly, 
transparently, and according to democratic principles, producing results that accurately 
reflect voter preferences (Norris, 2014). 
x. Voter Authentication: involves verifying that a person attempting to vote is eligible and 
authorized to participate in the election, typically through identity verification mechanisms 
(Jones & Simons, 2012). 
xi. Ballot Secrecy: ensures that individual votes cannot be traced back to specific voters, 
protecting voters from coercion and enabling free expression of political preferences 
(Benaloh, 2006). 
xii. End-to-End Verifiability : means that voters can verify their votes were correctly 
recorded, counted, and included in the final tally without compromising ballot secrecy 
(Adida, 2008). 
2.4 Methodologies and Research Designs 
Research in blockchain-based e-voting systems primarily uses experimental, design science, and 
case study methodologies. Experimental approaches test blockchain voting platforms to measure 
transaction throughput, latency, scalability, and security resilien ce against attack scenarios 
(Hjálmarsson et al., 2018). These studies compare blockchain implementations against traditional 
systems or evaluate different consensus mechanisms for optimal configurations. 
Design science research focuses on creating and evaluating new blockchain voting architectures 
for real-world electoral contexts (Kshetri & V oas, 2018). This involves iterative cycles of design, 
implementation, testing, and refinement based on stakeholder feedback. Case study methodologies 
examine actual deployments in Estonia, Switzerland, and Sierra Leone, documenting 
implementation challenges, voter acceptance rates, and operational outcomes (Hao et al., 2020). 
Mixed-methods approaches combine quantitati ve performance metrics with qualitative 
assessments of voter experiences and regulatory compliance (Pawlak et al., 2018). 
Data collection methods vary across studies. Some researchers use simulated elections with 
controlled populations, while others analyze data from pilot deployments or actual electoral events. 
Proof of Authority (PoA) and Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (PBFT) consensus mechanisms 
are popular choices for voting applications, offering transaction confirmation times under 10 
seconds compared to Proof of Work (Shahzad & Crowcroft, 2019). Ethereum and Hyperledger 
10 
 
Fabric dominate platform choices due to their mature development tools and extensive 
documentation. 
Evaluation frameworks consider technical performance, security, voter privacy, auditability, 
accessibility, and regulatory compliance (Springall et al., 2014). Security testing includes 
penetration testing, formal verification of smart contracts, and simulation of attack scenarios such 
as double-voting attempts, denial-of-service attacks, and node collusion. 
2.5 Empirical Studies 
Recent research demonstrates both the potential and challenges of blockchain -based e -voting 
systems. Bismark et al. (2025) pioneered NIN-blockchain integration in Nigeria, achieving 100% 
authentication accuracy by separating identity verification from vote recording.  
Njoku et al. (2023) used IBM Hyperledger Fabric with NIN authentication, showing that enterprise 
blockchains offer faster speeds and better privacy control than public blockchains. However, both 
studies tested only simulated systems without connecting to a ctual NIMC databases, leaving 
questions about real-world performance, network delays, and data reliability unanswered. 
Jayakumari et al. (2024) tackled scalability by combining cloud computing with blockchain 
technology, achieving fast transaction times through automated smart contracts. Their cloud-based 
approach raised concerns about data sovereignty and reliance on third-party providers.  
Osayomore et al. (2025) strengthened authentication by adding fingerprint and facial recognition 
to blockchain voting, demonstrating higher accuracy than NIN -only systems. However, the high 
costs of biometric hardware and privacy risks of centralized biome tric databases pose significant 
barriers to nationwide deployment. 
Steven et al. (2025) explored the Internet Computer Protocol in Indonesia, achieving over 1,000 
transactions per second at lower costs than Ethereum. Their real -world implementation revealed 
important challenges including voter education needs and varying technical literacy across age 
groups and locations. While valuable, their Indonesian -specific design lacks NIN integration and 
does not address Nigerian legal requirements, limiting direct application to Nigerian elections.

2.6 Conceptual Frameworks 
The conceptual framework for blockchain-based e-voting systems with NIN integration comprises 
interconnected layers. The identity layer manages voter registration, NIN verification, and 
biometric authentication to ensure only eligible citizens participate (Kshetri & V oas, 2018). This 
layer interfaces with Nigeria's National Identity Management Commission database to validate 
credentials before granting access. 
The blockchain layer provides distributed ledger infrastructure where votes are recorded as 
immutable transactions. Each vote is cryptographically hashed and linked to previous transactions, 
creating a tamper-evident chain (Yaga et al., 2019). Consensus mechanisms allow network nodes 
to agree on vote validity without central authority. 
The smart contract layer contains self -executing programs that enforce voting rules, validate 
eligibility, prevent double voting, and automatically tally results (Hjálmarsson et al., 2018). The 
security layer implements cryptographic encryption for confide ntiality, digital signatures for 
authentication, and access controls separating voter identity from vote content to maintain ballot 
secrecy (Shahzad & Crowcroft, 2019). 
The interface layer provides user-facing applications for voters, election officials, and observers, 
emphasizing simplicity for varying technical abilities (Risnanto & Imran, 2019). The verification 
layer enables end -to-end auditability, allowing voters to  confirm votes were recorded correctly 
while preventing identification of how individuals voted (Adida, 2008). 
This framework emphasizes modularity for independent component development and testing. 
Privacy-preserving design includes data minimization, ballot secrecy, and electoral law 
compliance. The model balances technological capabilities with democratic princi ples to achieve 
legitimacy (Norris, 2014). 
2.7 Debates and Controversies 
Significant debates surround the implementation of blockchain -based e -voting systems in 
democratic elections. Privacy concerns constitute the primary controversy. Critics argue that 
digital voting systems inherently create electronic trails that could link  voters to their ballot 
choices, compromising the fundamental democratic principle of secret ballots (Springall et al., 
2014). Opponents fear that government authorities or hackers could analyze voting patterns and 
12 
 
identify individual voters through transaction metadata, network analysis, or correlation with 
registration databases. 
1. Scalability challenges  present another contentious issue. Blockchain networks face 
throughput limitations that may prevent processing millions of votes within short 
timeframes during national elections (Shahzad & Crowcroft, 2019). Critics point to 
Ethereum's 15 -30 transactions per second as insufficient for large populations, though 
proponents argue that permissioned blockchains and layer -2 solutions can achieve 
necessary performance levels. 
2. Technological accessibility  raises concerns that blockchain voting may disadvantage 
citizens lacking internet connectivity, smartphones, or technical literacy, potentially 
creating digital divides that exclude vulnerable populations (Khan et al., 2021).  With 
researchers documenting exploitable weaknesses in smart contracts, client -side software, 
and network infrastructure that could enable vote manipulation despite blockchain's 
theoretical immutability (Atzei et al., 2017). 
3. The verifiability paradox creates philosophical tensions. Systems providing strong voter 
verification may inadvertently enable vote selling or coercion by allowing voters to prove 
how they voted to third parties (Benaloh, 2006). Regulatory uncertainty presents practical 
challenges, as most national electoral laws were written for paper -based systems and do 
not clearly address blockchain voting legality, recount procedures, or dispute resolution 
(Pawlak et al., 2018). 
4. Trust in technology versus trust in institutions remains fundamentally contested. Some 
argue that blockchain's cryptographic guarantees provide stronger assurances than human-
administered elections, while others maintain that democratic legitimacy requires human 
oversight that algorithms cannot replace (Norris, 2014). Cost-benefit calculations generate 
ongoing disagreement, with implementation requiring substantial upfront investments 
while cost savings materialize only over extended periods (Khan et al., 2021). 
5. Environmental impact  concerns have emerged regarding energy consumption, 
particularly for Proof of Work blockchains. These controversies have prompted varied 
regulatory responses globally, with some jurisdictions embracing blockchain voting pilots 
while others impose strict prohibitions.

2.8 Theoretical Contributions 
This research proposes an integrated framework combining blockchain technology with Nigeria's 
National Identification System to create a secure electoral architecture. The study demonstrates 
how distributed ledger immutability, cryptographic authentication, smart contracts, and biometric 
verification function as complementary security layers for democratic elections. By documenting 
design principles and trade-offs between transparency and privacy, the research provides insights 
applicable to broader governance contexts. 
The work contributes to democratic theory by exploring how technology -mediated voting differs 
from traditional ballot casting and its implications for political participation and electoral 
legitimacy in Nigeria. It extends the CIA Triad (Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability) framework 
by demonstrating its application where these principles must coexist despite inherent tensions, 
advancing zero-trust security models requiring continuous cryptographic verification. 
By examining NIN -based biometric authentication with blockchain anonymity, this research 
contributes to identity management theory  specifically how systems verify identity during 
authentication while preventing identity linkage during vote recording. The work demonstrates 
how smart contracts encode Nigerian electoral rules into executable logic.

CHAPTER THREE 
SYSTEM DESIGN AND METHODOLOGY 
3.1 Research Approach 
This research employs a mixed -methods approach, combining quantitative and qualitative 
elements to address the design, implementation, and evaluation of the blockchain -based e-voting 
system integrated with National Identification Number (NIN) authenticatio n. The quantitative 
component measures system performance through metrics such as transaction throughput (votes 
processed per second), authentication accuracy rates, blockchain synchronization latency, smart 
contract execution time, and system scalability under varying voter loads. Performance 
benchmarking compares the implemented system against existing electronic voting solutions in 
terms of vote recording speed, result tallying efficiency, and infrastructure resource consumption. 
The qualitative component includes evaluation of user experience through interface usability 
assessments, voter confidence surveys, and system functionality validation with stakeholders 
including potential voters, election officials, and technical administrators. User acceptance testing 
gathers feedback on interface clarity, authentication process simplicity, voting workflow 
intuitiveness, and overall trust in the system's ability to record votes accurately and securely. 
This mixed -methods approach provides a complete understanding of both the technical 
performance capabilities and practical usability of the developed system, ensuring that the solution 
addresses Nigeria's electoral integrity challenges while remaining acce ssible and trustworthy for 
end users across diverse demographic groups and technical literacy levels. 
3.2 Research Design 
This research uses experimental design and design science methodology. The study designs, 
implements, and tests the blockchain e -voting system to evaluate its effectiveness. The research 
has four stages: architecture design, prototype development, function al testing, and performance 
evaluation. 
The architecture design phase selects the blockchain platform, determines the consensus 
mechanism, specifies smart contract logic, and establishes NIN integration protocols. The 
prototype development phase builds core components incrementally: voter regist ration module, 
19 
 
NIN authentication interface, blockchain vote recording mechanism, and smart contract -based 
tallying. 
Functional testing simulates electoral processes including voter registration, authentication 
attempts (legitimate and fraudulent), vote casting, and result tallying. This verifies the system 
enforces electoral rules and prevents unauthorized actions. Perf ormance evaluation measures 
authentication accuracy, transaction processing speed, scalability under voter loads, and 
blockchain synchronization efficiency. Usability testing with representative voter demographics 
assesses user experience. 
This design validates whether the system addresses Nigeria's electoral integrity challenges while 
remaining practical and accessible for real-world deployment. 
3.3 Software Development Methodology 
This project adopts an adapted Agile methodology, specifically Scrum, due to the iterative nature 
of blockchain smart contract development, NIN integration testing, and the need for continuous 
security validation and refinement. 
3.3.1 Adopted Methodology 
An adapted Agile (Scrum) methodology was selected for this project based on several project -
specific factors: 
i. Iterative Blockchain Development : Smart contracts for vote recording, authentication, 
and tallying require continuous refinement. Agile's sprint-based approach enables iterative 
improvement of security, efficiency, and compliance. 
ii. Evolving Security Requirements : Security vulnerabilities discovered during 
development require requirement updates. Agile accommodates these changes without 
complete project restructuring. 
iii. Early Component Testing: Each sprint delivers functional modules (NIN authentication, 
blockchain vote recording, smart contract execution). This enables early testing before full 
integration, allowing rapid issue identification and correction. 
iv. Complexity Management: The system is broken into manageable features across sprints: 
voter registration, authentication interface, vote casting, and result tallying. This reduces 
complexity and enables focused development.

v. Academic Timeline Constraints: This final year project has strict submission deadlines. 
Agile sprints ensure consistent progress within the required timeframe. 
The Scrum implementation uses 2 -week sprint cycles. Each sprint focuses on specific system 
components. Sprint planning defines objectives and deliverables. Daily tracking monitors 
progress. Sprint reviews evaluate outputs against acceptance criteria. Sprint retrospectives identify 
process improvements. 
3.4 Methodology: Tools and System Architecture 
1. Development Stack The system is built on the Ethereum ecosystem. Solidity is used for 
smart contract logic, while the Truffle Suite and Ganache provide the framework for 
development, testing, and local blockchain emulation. For the interface, React.js 
(Frontend) and Node.js/Express (Backend) handle the voter experience and API requests, 
respectively. 
2. Authentication and Security To verify identity without compromising privacy, the system 
integrates the WebAuthn API. This allows for device -level biometric authentication 
(fingerprint or facial recognition) where sensitive data never leaves the user's device. For 
data integrity, AES -256 encrypts voter information, while Elliptic Curve Cryptography 
(ECC) ensures secure digital signatures for every cast vote. 
 
3.4.1 Core Technical Tools 
i. Blockchain: Solidity, Web3.js, OpenZeppelin (Security libraries). 
ii. Database: MongoDB for registration logs; a simulated NIN Database  for NIMC 
verification. 
iii. Security: JWT for session management; bcrypt for hashing credentials. 
iv. Testing: Mocha/Chai for smart contracts; Postman for API validation.

3.4.2 System Architecture and Workflow 
The integration of NIN and Blockchain follows a four -stage process to ensure "one person, one 
vote." 
1. NIN Verification: The voter enters their 11 -digit NIN. The system queries the simulated 
NIMC database to confirm eligibility and checks the blockchain ledger to ensure the user 
hasn't already registered. 
2. Biometric Enrollment (WebAuthn):  First-time users link their NIN to their device’s 
biometrics. WebAuthn generates a cryptographic key pair; the biometric data stays on the 
device, while the public key is stored for future authentication. 
3. Authentication & Vote Casting:  For subsequent logins, the system issues a biometric 
challenge. Upon a successful scan, the voter accesses the ballot. The selected vote is 
encrypted and sent as a blockchain transaction. 
4. Immutability & Double-Voting Prevention: The smart contract maps a hashed version 
of the NIN to a "voted" status. Once a vote is recorded on the distributed ledger, the 
blockchain's immutability prevents any alteration, and the system automatically rejects 
further attempts from that NIN.

3.6 Data Analysis and Evaluation Plan 
Performance Metrics 
The system's effectiveness is measured through four primary indicators: 
1. Authentication Accuracy: The percentage of legitimate NIN holders correctly verified via 
WebAuthn versus rejected unauthorized attempts. 
2. Transaction Speed: The time elapsed from vote submission to blockchain confirmation. 
3. Security Integrity:  The rate at which the smart contract successfully blocks duplicate 
voting attempts. 
4. System Throughput: The capacity to handle concurrent voters without performance lag. 
Evaluation Method 
The system is evaluated using controlled test cases with predetermined outcomes to verify 
technical and security requirements. 
Test Case Categories 
1. Legitimate Scenarios:  Verifies NIN-biometric registration, concurrent voting sessions, 
and post-vote verification. 
2. Security Violations: Tests the system’s ability to reject invalid NINs, block duplicate votes 
via smart contracts, and deny access upon biometric failure. 
3. Edge Cases: Assesses system resilience during network interruptions, biometric timeouts, 
and cross-browser compatibility. 
3.7 Validity and Reliability 
To ensure research integrity, the following measures are implemented: 
1. Internal Validity: Controlled scenarios with predefined inputs and outputs eliminate 
external variables. Using a simulated NIN database ensures that system behaviors reflect 
design logic rather than external network fluctuations. 
24 
 
2. External Validity: Testing covers realistic electoral conditions, including varying voter 
loads (single vs. peak concurrent users), diverse devices (mobile vs. desktop), and different 
network latencies to ensure findings apply to real-world deployment. 
3. Reliability: The deterministic nature of Smart Contracts ensures that identical inputs 
always produce identical outputs. Repeated testing of authentication workflows confirms 
consistent acceptance or rejection of credentials. 
4. Measurement & Construct Validity:  Metrics like Authentication Accuracy and 
Transaction Time are benchmarked against existing blockchain literature. These criteria 
accurately measure the system's ability to enforce electoral rules and maintain 
responsiveness.
